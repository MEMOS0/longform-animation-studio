## Hit 1: The Giants' Wall — extraction vs the living boundary

The opening is culturally grounded ordinary labor before mythology: eggs/bread, charcoal production, mountain paths, old stones, shepherding, a family ironworks, communal forest ownership. At 00:00 the first image is already scale and material mystery — a giant hand near stones — but the next minutes root the legend in work and land use rather than pure spectacle.

Machine-transcribed evidence indicates that the initial conflict is enclosure/resource extraction, not a generic villain attack:

- 00:00 — “Who could carry stones like these?”
- 00:30 — “They've come back after 60 years.”
- 01:13 — “The lower woods are gone, Father.”
- 01:35–01:42 — “Then we take the high oaks.” / “Those belong to the whole village.”
- 01:64–01:70 — silver is offered “for every man who cuts for me,” while another voice answers, “Those oaks hold up the mountain.”
- 01:90–01:93 — “He wants the high oaks… Without the oaks, the big ones leave.”

The giants are not pets or collectible creatures. Their presence is coupled to a boundary system: stones, walls, communal land, memory, and ecological stability. The human antagonism is also material: paid labor, moved boundary stones, charcoal demand, ironworks inheritance, and land theft.

Mid-film evidence:

- 08:47 — “They moved the boundary into the oaks.”
- 09:59 — “The stones say these oaks are mine.”
- 09:80–09:97 — accusation and counter-knowledge: “You move them in the night” versus “Those walls are older than all of us.”
- 10:36 — an order to “watch their fold tonight and tear it down.”
- 11:13–11:40 — the giant/shepherd absence becomes communal guilt: “Where are you? Please come back.” / “He's gone because of us.”
- 12:63 — relational cost: “Please don't go. Not yet.”
- 12:93 — confession/evidence enters: “My father moved the stones. I have proof.”
- 13:21 — ecological causality is explicit: “Without roots the whole slope will slide.”

The climax is physical, not abstract: at 14:06 “Its roots hold the whole slope”; at 14:28 “Cut this and the mountain comes down”; at 14:68 “Wake the village”; at 14:88 “The slope is coming down”; at 15:23 the father still prioritizes charcoal (“Save the charcoal”), while the younger generation answers “Leave it father.” The ending uses repair rather than conquest: the father publicly admits he “paid men to move the stones” and “strip the slope above you” (16:91–17:18), the giant returns/carries the old man home, and the final visual movement is toward wall, dawn, snow, eggs, and continuity.

**Doctrine:** ecological legends work when the supernatural is infrastructural — a giant is part of the boundary/commons system — and when extraction produces visible physical consequences.

## Near-zero comparator: The Half-Orc — strong images, generic contract

The 83-view film has polished fantasy imagery and a coherent political-betrayal plot. Uploaded subtitle evidence:

- opening raid: “Take the grain and the steel. Leave the people.” / “Hold the bridge.”
- identity wound: “You're one of us.” / “I'm nothing.”
- suspicion/betrayal: “Who told you the gate of Harrowmere would be open?”
- both-sides manipulation: “The humans filled our wells with stone… Someone is lying to both of us.”
- political logic: “In war, no one may challenge the chieftain. That is why he needs his war.”
- ending pressure: “The half orc lives.” / “Not after dawn.”

This is not incoherent. But compared with the folk hits, its contract is a familiar fantasy-war/identity/political-betrayal mechanism. Contact sheets emphasize raids, fire, warriors, riders, chieftains, and burning settlements. It lacks the hits' specific ecological object-system: wall/oaks/water/herbs/honey/boundary/seasonal memory. The supernatural body is an identity/war body rather than an ecological commons relation.

**Anti-overfit conclusion:** low discovery does not prove weak writing. The useful distinction is not “good versus bad”; it is “culturally specific reciprocal ecological legend” versus “generic fantasy war machinery.”

## Production doctrine — folk ecological reciprocity

1. Ground the legend in ordinary material labor before magic: food, rope, charcoal, farming, harvesting, water, herbs, craft, doors, hearths.
2. Make the supernatural entity part of ecological or communal infrastructure: wall, boundary, spring, forest, medicine, memory, seasonal door.
3. Use extraction/enclosure/broken reciprocity as the central conflict: cutting commons trees, moving stones, diverting water, capturing a healing being, turning a heart into immortality.
4. Give the human mediator a bounded role: witness, oathkeeper, healer's student, child of the village, inheritor of a harmful family trade — not owner of the creature.
5. Make betrayal costly socially and physically: hunters arrive, mother is threatened, slope slides, water disappears, spring door closes.
6. Let ordinary evidence expose human lies: moved stones, ditches, nail marks, poison herbs, confession, witnesses.
7. Restore the commons rather than reward the hero with possession. The creature goes home, refuses palace ownership, or remains autonomous.
8. End with memory/continuity: dawn, spring, wall, water returning, forget-me-not, communal doors warm.
9. Packaging should name cultural legend identity and show one relational creature/environment image. Avoid crowded generic warrior ensembles unless war is truly the differentiating promise.
10. Do not treat low discovery as proof of weak story; Kanlaon and Half-Orc are counterexamples with real craft.
