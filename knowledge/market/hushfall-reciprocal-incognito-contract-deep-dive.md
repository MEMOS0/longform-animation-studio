## Timestamped story mechanism

### 00:00-05:00 — compressed gender-inverted contract hook

The film opens inside a private plane with a succession deadline: “Married by Friday or Uncle Martin gets my chair.” Audrey Whitfield interrogates Lucas Ward — name, age, net worth — and concludes “Complicated means broke. Perfect. Broke men sign anything.” She offers a one-year contract: `$100,000 a month, no touching, no feelings.` Lucas refuses because he is flying home to marry someone else. Audrey assumes love is a terrible investment; Lucas answers, “Depends who you invest in.”

The same opening introduces Iron Crest Capital, the fund whose founder nobody has seen. Lucas is visually coded as relaxed but not obviously poor; Audrey is coded as hyper-controlled corporate power. The plane is not just a meet-cute location: it is a sealed negotiation chamber where both characters test status assumptions before either can perform for an audience.

### 05:00-15:00 — broken engagement, corporate coercion, and counter-contract

Lucas is publicly humiliated by the fiancée/family system he was returning to. Audrey's need remains institutional, not merely romantic: if she loses the board vote, Martin sells Whitfield in pieces. Lucas is investigated as a possible con man while also proving he reads fine print and understands capital structure. After losing his fiancée, he accepts Audrey's marriage need with two conditions: he does not want her money and she must not ask who he is.

This reverses the usual contract-marriage power relation twice: Audrey appears to buy a husband, but Lucas refuses the price; Audrey appears to control the contract, but Lucas controls the identity information.

### 15:00-25:00 — plane object-memory and governance stakes

The plane recurs as object-memory and moral test. Lucas “stole” her laptop on a plane; he promised `$100 billion` on a plane. He claims he gets a pasta company out of helping her — a deliberately trivial, human-scale answer inside a billion-dollar corporate conflict. The contract is exposed to the grandmother/family system, including a clause that the husband shall not fall in love with the wife. Audrey is accused of buying a husband to lie; the film makes contract artifice morally costly instead of merely cute.

### 25:00-35:00 — gala humiliation and concealed-founder dramatic irony

At the Iron Crest gala Lucas is treated as a servant or “box boy.” Audrey initially asks him to leave because he does not belong. The gala's empty founder's chair intensifies the hidden-founder irony. Public class humiliation is not simply cruelty: it exposes how both leads participate in status sorting even when they resent it.

### 35:00-45:00 — mutual concealment becomes mutual recognition

The reveal is not merely “poor man is rich.” Lucas explains that if he had said he owned Iron Crest, Audrey would have called security; more importantly, he would never know whether anyone could love him with nothing. Audrey recognizes the inverse problem: eleven men proposed to her in a year, and every one was effectively proposing to Whitfield Group. Their wealth creates mirrored epistemic wounds — neither can know whether desire is for the person or the institution.

The emotional closure also revises the opening contract. Lucas admits he broke the no-feelings clause “before the plane even landed.” He fell not simply for the woman but for “the woman brave enough to ask” for an impossible sum. Evidence/object `C2A` reframes the plane as proof that the best thing in his life happened there. The final movement shifts from institutional victory to chosen intimacy: “The plane had better plans.”

## Why this is novel relative to the existing doctrines

This is not just contract marriage and not just hidden billionaire. The reusable mechanism is **reciprocal incognito class epistemology**:

- The female CEO begins with apparent economic power and uses a contract to control intimacy.
- The male lead appears economically weak but secretly controls even greater capital.
- Both concealment positions are motivated by the same epistemic problem: wealth prevents knowledge of unconditional love.
- The contract's no-feelings clause fails almost immediately; the story's tension is not whether love appears but whether love can become informed and mutual after asymmetric knowledge.
- Luxury institutions (Whitfield/Iron Crest) are both prize and epistemic prison.
- The plane functions as a recurring neutral chamber/object-memory where the relationship begins outside normal corporate hierarchy and is re-read at the ending.
- The ending chooses humility/ordinariness rather than simply upgrading the couple into richer power.
