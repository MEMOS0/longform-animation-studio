## Transferable doctrine
1. **Miracles must create ethical temptation.** Pearl tears are valuable because they cost suffering. If magic is free money, the melodrama collapses.
2. **Care before monetization.** “Pearls can wait. She can't” is the exact ordering that makes later economic use morally complicated rather than greedy.
3. **Fantasy creature + debt/housing can share one engine.** Both are ownership threats: the house is collateral and the child is “living treasure.”
4. **Legal proof can coexist with creature fantasy.** “Truth was written down” works because documents, debt, object provenance, and public institutions are already part of the world.
5. **The villain’s deepest crime is category violence.** Calling Pearl “no child” and “creature” is worse than theft because it denies personhood.
6. **Return-home can be loving separation.** Pearl belongs to the ocean and her mer-family; the payoff is not adoption/possession but safe return plus enduring chosen bond.
7. **Epilogue should repay the opening economic wound spatially.** Eviction/debt becomes owned land, garden, water, honest business, partnership, and dignity “never on the menu.”
8. **Vertical long-form can retain through episodic micro-objectives.** The two-hour runtime is carried by repeating concrete problems — debt deadline, hiding, pearl sale, legal freeze, road escape, ocean return — each with local resolution and escalation.

## Production implications
- When combining creature fantasy with social melodrama, map the same ownership logic across both systems: home, body, labor, object, child/creature.
- Give magical objects a cost and a consent rule before they become economic solutions.
- Use food/cart/customer economies as visible proof of capability, not just poverty texture.
- In vertical format, keep hands, food, documents, blanket, tears, pearls, and child faces large and readable.
- End creature-return stories with transformed human space; otherwise the human protagonist’s original wound remains unpaid.
